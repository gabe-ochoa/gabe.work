I lead Transactional Storage at Datadog, running the application data platforms that sit under every Datadog product: Postgres, key-value databases, search, replication, and caching.

I came out of Georgia Tech as a mechanical engineer and I wanted to build physical things, so I went to Quirky and spent my days prototyping for a four-person team that reported to the CEO. From there I moved to Wink, where I spent a year taking fifty-odd partner products apart to work out why they didn't do what they promised. Somewhere in the middle of all that I started actually writing code, which was not the plan. Then I was running the infrastructure underneath it at Squarespace, then all of engineering at Numina, and eventually leading the teams that build the thing instead of building it myself. The title changed every couple of years. The work didn't: take it apart, figure out how it actually works, put it back together better.

I'm still happiest where software meets the physical world, where the data is about something that is genuinely happening out there rather than a number that only exists in a database. And I care a great deal about being the kind of manager people do their best work for, which is harder to put a number on than anything else on this page.

How I work

Build with customers.

Data Streams went from pre-revenue to multi-million-dollar ARR in about a year, and the reason wasn't that we were especially clever. We sat next to the people using it and let their complaints set the roadmap, which is slower to feel good and much faster to work. A data-warehouse monitoring prototype got its first paying customer four weeks after two engineers started on it, the same way.

Stay close to the computers.

I read the code, I sit in the incident channel, and I still ship my own products on nights and weekends. Not because a manager needs to be writing production code, but because the ones who drift too far from the work start making worse calls about it and are usually the last to notice.

There's always a way.

A rewrite of a storage engine had stalled out for long enough that people had stopped expecting it, and it shipped to GA once we reset it. A Postgres fleet moved across six data centers in under four weeks. Neither of those turned out to be a technology problem when we got to the bottom of them, and focus is a thing you can actually go fix.

Execute short, aim long.

Be ruthless about what ships this quarter while keeping a clear view of where the thing is supposed to end up in three years. Ship it, learn from it, adjust, and run the loop again until it sticks.

First 90 days anywhere, the order is always the same: meet the customers, read the code, find the money, and ship something small enough that it actually ships.
